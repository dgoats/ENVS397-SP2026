Germantown in Context

What does it mean to live with flooding?

Photo: Philadelphia Water Department

What does it mean to live with flooding? What if that flooding isn't the result of a one-time "natural disaster" but a chronic condition – the by-product of decades of disinvestment, structural racism, and inadequate prioritization? In that case, how might concerned community members intervene and take control of their neighborhood's future infrastructure?

Germantown has long been on the front lines of urban flooding, and of policies that distribute its effects unequally. This semester, Haverford ENVS 397 students worked alongside The Water Shed and Germantown residents to explore how pluvial flooding impacts the neighborhood and what community-rooted solutions could look like.

The five research projects below range from a neighborhood climate resilience plan and a degrowth strategy for unbuilding infrastructure, to curricula for local youth, and a food web map of the Bi-Co. Each offers a different entry point into what a more resilient Germantown could look like.

Infrastructure · Degrowth · Justice

Germantown
Just Transition / Degrowth PLAN

Drawing on just transition and degrowth frameworks, this project proposes strategic alternatives to conventional flood management: depaving, soil restoration, and community-controlled approaches to building a more porous, water-ready Germantown.

Education · Youth · Flooding

Germantown Flooding Curriculum

A place-based environmental education curriculum teaching Germantown youth to understand, interpret, and respond to the pluvial flooding shaping their neighborhood. This project connects local water dynamics to broader histories of disinvestment and community resilience.

↓ Thesis (PDF) ↗ Slides (Link) ▶ Video

Jo Smith


"Although the planning required for educators to implement place-based learning, such as building connections with community members and identifying locations, can be high, researchers have found that, when compared to a more traditional lecture-based classroom, there is significantly higher student engagement and understanding of course content." - Dluzniewski, Ring, Smith (2026).

Education · Youth · Water Quality

Germantown Water Quality Curriculum

A 5th and 6th grade curriculum focused on water quality in Germantown that examines both what's in the water, and how residents can become informed participants in monitoring, advocacy, and the promotion of environmental justice in their community.

Ecology · Food Systems · Mapping

Toward a Better Bi-Co Food Web

An ecological and socio-responsive mapping of how food flows through the Bryn Mawr–Haverford campus ecosystem — from sourcing and procurement to waste. This project leverages GIS mapping and collaborative visualizations of institutional foodways as a model for community-scale food system mapping.

About the Capstone

ENVS 397
Senior Capstone in Environmental Studies
↗ Syllabus (link)

ENVS 397 is the culminating capstone course for Bi-Co Environmental Studies majors. The course is structured as a semester-long collaboration with a community partner, with which students develop meaningful, collaborative, place-based projects. In this case, we worked with The Water Shed, a Germantown-based environmental organization that operates a storefront community hub focused on flooding, environmental resilience, stormwater, and neighborhood-scale climate adaptation.

In a typical semester, students work in interdisciplinary teams to develop applied research projects that respond to the needs of our community partner(s). These may take a variety of approaches and methodologies, drawing on the students' individual and collective backgrounds, and could include resilience planning, economic assessments, curriculum development, oral histories, outreach strategies, or the creation of public-facing resources.

For more on interdisciplinary, project-based learning, see also: Rangel, "Why Interdisciplinary Project-Based Learning? Assessing the Benefits and Challenges at U.S. Institutions of Higher Education"
